## 1. Why Peace Could Not Be Built

Across Myanmar’s modern history, successive peace attempts failed because of period-specific policy gaps and fundamentally incompatible definitions of “peace.” Three distinct eras reveal the pattern:

| Era | Formula | Outcome |
|-----|---------|---------|
| **1948 – 1988** | Security without politics | Political problems treated as purely military problems; durable peace never achieved. |
| **1989 – 2010** | Ceasefire without political settlement | Ceasefires obtained, but without binding political agreements; arrangements later collapsed. |
| **2011 – 2020** | Process without political outcome | Peace process existed, yet simultaneous political results, mutual trust, and implementing institutions were not built. |

### Divergent Narratives of “Peace”

**Military narrative:**  
The state is permanently vulnerable to fragmentation. Peace is therefore defined as “State Survival.” Ethnic Armed Organisations (EAOs) are framed primarily as security threats.

**EAO narrative:**  
Peace requires political equality, recognition, and a federal redesign of the state itself. The goal is not merely the cessation of fighting, but the reconstruction of the political order.

Until these narratives converge on a shared definition of the political end-state, technical peace processes remain structurally incomplete.

## 2. Military and Power-Apparatus Shifts (2021 – 2026)

### Territory and Force Balance

Over five years the military lost significant territory, especially after Operation 1027, and was forced to fight on multiple fronts simultaneously. According to **ISP Myanmar (21 July 2026)**, the military still does not control **81 towns** and has relinquished approximately **39.34 %** of the country’s land area.

### Force Attrition and Air Dependence

Combat strength is estimated to have fallen by roughly **two-thirds** through casualties, desertion and defections. As ground capability declined, reliance on airstrikes intensified. **USIP (12 August 2026)** recorded more than **3,000 airstrikes** in the period under review.

### Why the Military Has Not Collapsed Rapidly

Despite territorial and manpower losses, the military retains several residual strengths:

- Continued control of the major urban centres (Naypyitaw, Yangon, Mandalay)
- Functional air force and heavy-weapons capability
- Operational central administration and banking mechanisms
- Sustained military-technology imports valued at over **USD 1 billion** from Russia, China, India, Thailand and Singapore

UN reporting characterises the military as a **“weakened yet still-functioning power apparatus”** — degraded, but not yet dislodged from the core instruments of state control.

## 3. Revolutionary Coordination and Socio-Economic Impact

### Political–Military Integration

On **7 August 2026** the Federal Democracy Union Leadership Council and the Military Strategy Coordination and Command Committee jointly established mechanisms designed to link political direction with military operations — an explicit attempt to move from fragmented resistance toward coordinated revolutionary architecture.

### Economic and Livelihood Collapse

The parallel market rate of the kyat reached approximately **7,500 per US dollar**. **World Bank data (12 July 2026)** indicate that more than **40 %** of the population has fallen below the poverty line.

### Human-Capital Drain

The conscription law accelerated the exodus of young people. The resulting long-term human-capital loss constitutes a structural liability for any future reconstruction.

### Collapse of Public Trust

**AAPP figures (17 August 2026)** record nearly **40,000 arrests** and more than **8,200 killings**. **UNHCR data (10 August 2026)** place the number of internally displaced and refugee persons above **four million**. The social contract between the population and the military authority has been comprehensively ruptured.

## 4. Concluding Assessment — The New Decisive Variables

The outcome is no longer determined solely by “who captures more towns.” Four interlocking variables now decide the trajectory:

| # | Decisive Variable | Analytical Question |
|---|-------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | **Military sustainment** | Can manpower and ammunition supply be maintained over a prolonged horizon? |
| 2 | **Revolutionary coordination** | How effective is the political–military integration of resistance forces? |
| 3 | **Governance in controlled areas** | Can public services and administration be built more effectively than the previous order? |
| 4 | **International policy shifts** | How do external actors adjust material and diplomatic support over time? |

### Sentinel Principle — Classroom 42

> **The decisive question is no longer “Who is stronger today?”**  
> **It is “Who can endure longer — and, having endured, construct a better political and administrative order?”**
