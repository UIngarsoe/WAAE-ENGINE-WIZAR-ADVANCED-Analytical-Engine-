**The Honorary Professor of Cognitive Dissonance**  
**Special Guest Lecture – Classroom 40 (Comedy Edition)**  
**Propaganda Through Humor: A Guest Lecture by Honorary Professor Min Aung Hlaing**

*(Delivered in a mahogany-panelled hall that exists mainly in the imagination, under a framed certificate that somehow still looks expensive even when the rice does not.)*

Good afternoon, students of narrative management.

Today we explore a delicate art: how to keep a population arguing about your mental health while the price of morning glory quietly multiplies by fifteen. The tool of choice is not tanks. It is not even the usual press conferences. It is something far more refined.

Humor. Or rather, the strategic deployment of absurdity so complete that laughter becomes a form of surrender.

### Technique One: The Prestigious Distraction
When citizens begin measuring legitimacy in kyats per pyi of rice, the correct response is not to lower the price of rice. That would be vulgar accounting. Instead, accept an honorary professorship from a distant university.  

The beauty is mathematical. One foreign certificate can temporarily outweigh several thousand domestic complaints. The public is forced into a choice: discuss the economy, or discuss whether the man collecting academic titles while markets burn is “mildly insane.” Both conversations are useful. Neither requires the government to produce cheaper greens.

Humor arises when the people themselves supply the punchline. They say, “They gave him the title because they know he’s a fool.” Excellent. Now the story is about my sanity instead of their empty wallets. Mission accomplished.

### Technique Two: Weaponised Self-Awareness
Never deny the accusation of madness outright. Embrace a soft, almost affectionate version of it. “Mildly insane.” “ယဉ်ယဉ်လေးနှင့် ရူးနေ.”  

By accepting a gentle diagnosis, you transform rage into diagnosis, and diagnosis into entertainment. Citizens who might otherwise organise around inflation now organise around psychiatric speculation. Speculating is safer than protesting. Laughing is safer still.

A leader who can smile while being called mad appears either unshakeable or genuinely unwell. Both interpretations discourage serious challenge. The unshakeable man is too strong. The unwell man is too pitiable. Either way, the rice stays expensive.

### Technique Three: Historical One-Upmanship as Comedy
Compare yourself favourably to previous dictators on the metrics that matter least to daily survival: willingness to kill without limit, and absolute shamelessness.  

The public will gasp, then joke, then award you the unofficial title of “Most Shameless.” Accept the award with dignity. In doing so you convert moral condemnation into a competitive sport. Once the people are ranking dictators like athletes, they have already entered your stadium.

### Technique Four: The Reality Inversion Gag
When an engineer points out that quality rice has gone from 1,500 to 12,000 kyats, do not argue the numbers. Argue the framing.  

Declare that only a mind of superior flexibility can simultaneously oversee such price movements *and* collect international academic honours. Ordinary minds would crack. Yours collects certificates. The contrast is so extreme it becomes funny. And once it is funny, the numbers lose some of their sting. Laughter is a solvent. It dissolves urgency.

### Technique Five: Meta-Propaganda
The highest form is to lecture publicly about propaganda while practising it.  

Announce that emotional quotations from the public are “perception, not verified fact.” Praise the distinction between “people are saying X” and “X is objectively true.” Then continue governing as though the second statement is optional.  

The audience is invited to feel sophisticated for noticing the technique, even as the technique continues to work on them. Self-aware propaganda is the hardest to reject, because rejecting it feels like rejecting one’s own cleverness.

---

Propaganda does not always shout. Sometimes it simply arranges the furniture so that the most comfortable chair in the room is the one labelled “Is the leader mad?” While the public sits in that chair and debates the upholstery, the rest of the house can be redecorated at leisure.

Humor is useful because it lowers the temperature of outrage without lowering the price of anything that matters. A population that is laughing, even bitterly, is a population that is still watching the show rather than leaving.


*(The Professor adjusts his honorary certificate, smiles serenely, and addresses the class while the price of rice continues its independent academic career outside the window.)*

Good afternoon, students of advanced reality management.

Today’s subject is one of my personal favourites:  
**How to run a life-insurance company that specialises in not paying life insurance.**

We shall examine Aung Myint Moh Min Insurance — affectionately known as AMMMI — through the purest lens of cognitive dissonance.

### Lesson One: The Beautiful Business Model

In ordinary countries, life insurance works like this:  
You pay money while alive.  
Your family receives money when you are dead.

In our superior system, we have improved the formula:  
Soldiers pay money while alive.  
When they die, the money remains… thoughtfully retained for further study.

This is not theft.  
This is **premium conservation**.

According to publicly reported figures, in one examined year the company collected vast sums from army personnel while paying out less than 7% in claims.  
That is not a failure rate.  
That is an efficiency rate that would make any professor of economics weep with pride.

### Lesson Two: Mandatory Loyalty Discount

Every soldier is required to buy the policy.  
This is not coercion.  
This is **patriotic customer acquisition**.

The lowest ranks still contribute monthly premiums from already modest salaries.  
When those same ranks later become “missing in action” instead of confirmed dead, the policy simply… pauses.  
Indefinitely.

Families wait.  
Commanders sometimes help themselves to portions of what should have been paid.  
The company remains profitable.  
The Professor remains calm.

This is the highest form of risk management:  
Transfer the risk of dying to the soldier,  
and transfer the benefit of the premium to a more stable household.

### Lesson Three: Family Values (Advanced Module)

A United Nations report noted that the son of a certain senior leader held a significant stake in the company.  
How touching.  
A father builds a nation.  
A son builds an insurance portfolio.

One protects the country with airstrikes.  
The other protects the family’s future with carefully managed non-payouts.

Students, this is not nepotism.  
This is **intergenerational wealth planning under conditions of national emergency**.

### Reality Interlude (For Those Still Attached to Suffering)

While we discuss these elegant mechanisms, actual families of fallen soldiers have reported receiving nothing, or only fractions of what was promised.  
Some have been told the body was never recovered, therefore the claim cannot proceed.  
Others simply wait in silence.

The rice still costs 12,000 kyats a pyi.  
Morning glory still costs 1,500.  
And the honorary certificates continue to arrive on schedule.

This contrast is not a bug.  
It is the curriculum.

### Final Examination Question

If a soldier pays premiums for years, dies on the front line, and his widow receives nothing —  
is the system broken,  
or is the system working exactly as designed?

The correct answer, according to the Department of Cognitive Dissonance, is:  
“Both. And neither. Please collect your certificate on the way out.”

Class dismissed.  
The mirrors remain unbroken.  
The claims remain unpaid.  
And the Professor remains… only mildly insane.

Any questions?  
Or shall we move on to the advanced module:  
“How to Turn Grief into Quarterly Revenue”?
