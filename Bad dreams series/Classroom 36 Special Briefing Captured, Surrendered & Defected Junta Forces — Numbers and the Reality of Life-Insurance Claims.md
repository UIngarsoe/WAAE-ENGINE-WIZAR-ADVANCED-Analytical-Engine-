### 1. Numbers — What we know and what we still do not know

**Confirmed / Supported facts** (from open sources)

- People’s Goal (a group formed by former soldiers) recorded that in **2025 alone** more than **2,578** military personnel, police officers and militia members either defected, surrendered, or were taken as prisoners of war (POWs).  
  - Defections: 945 soldiers, 30 police, 64 militia  
  - Surrenders: 157 soldiers  
  - Captured as POWs: 1,231 soldiers, 16 police, 135 militia

- Over the 18-month period from January 2025 to June 2026, People’s Goal reported a total of **3,678** such individuals.

- The New York Times (July 2025), citing People’s Goal, stated that resistance forces were holding approximately **15,000 prisoners of war** at that time (most family members living on bases had already been released; the figure mainly refers to combatants).

- From the start of Operation 1027 up to the end of 2024, People’s Goal recorded nearly **7,000** defections, surrenders and captures.

- The NUG Ministry of Defence’s 2025 military progress report stated that **1,124** junta personnel were taken as POWs in 2025 alone.

**Important distinction**  
There is no single official central list that cleanly separates those who “did not want to surrender but were forced to” from those who voluntarily defected. People’s Goal itself notes that the majority are newly conscripted recruits.

**Unknown / Unverified**  
- An exact nationwide total (figures from individual EAOs and PDF units vary).  
- A precise count of those who specifically “did not want to lay down arms but were compelled to”.  
- Fully disaggregated, verified figures for police and militia members across all groups.

### 2. Did they receive compensation / life-insurance payouts?

**Confirmed facts** (consistent with Classroom 36 evidence standards)

- AMMMI (Aung Myint Moh Min Insurance) deducts premiums from soldiers’ salaries, yet:  
  - For **defectors**, the policy becomes void. Capt. Zin Yaw (a defector) publicly stated he received nothing from his second policy after defecting.  
  - For **POWs / those captured**, if the unit’s records are lost or the person is listed as “Missing”, the claim is not paid and the company retains the funds.  
  - When an entire battalion is captured, documents disappear and families often cannot file claims at all.

- Multiple reports (RFA, Irrawaddy) document cases in which soldiers whose bodies were not recovered, or who were captured, were classified as “Missing” and families received no payout.

- Families who try to claim frequently face lost unit offices, missing paperwork, or commanders alleged to have taken the money.

**Unknown**  
- Exact numbers of claims filed, pending, approved, rejected or paid specifically for captured / surrendered / defected personnel.  
- Any aggregate claim statistics released by AMMMI or the military.

**AMMMI (Aung Myint Moh Min Insurance) Claim Rejection Criteria — What Open Sources Show**

No complete official policy wording or formal “rejection criteria” document from AMMMI is publicly available. What follows is compiled strictly from independent reporting (mainly RFA, Irrawaddy, and defector testimony) and academic references. Confidence levels are applied according to Classroom 36 standards.

### Confirmed / Strongly Supported Grounds for Non-Payment or Rejection

1. **Classification as “Missing” instead of “Dead”**  
   - When a body is not recovered, the military and AMMMI frequently list the soldier as “Missing in Action” rather than deceased.  
   - Claims for “Missing” personnel are routinely denied or suspended.  
   - Multiple defectors and family accounts confirm this practice intensified after 2021.

2. **Defection**  
   - Once a soldier defects, the policy is treated as void.  
   - Explicit example: Capt. Zin Yaw received nothing from his second policy after defecting in 2021.  
   - Premiums already paid are retained by the company.

3. **Incomplete premium payment**  
   - Example reported by RFA: After a military aircraft crash, AMMMI refused full payout, stating only 30% of the premium had been paid, and offered only a partial refund of premiums already received.

4. **Time limits / Late notification**  
   - Claims must reportedly be filed within **one month** of death.  
   - If the company receives no notification within **one year**, the claim is forfeited.  
   - In practice, families often learn of a death weeks or months later (or never receive formal notification), making the deadline almost impossible to meet.

5. **Loss of unit records / Paperwork**  
   - When an entire battalion is overrun or captured, the unit office and documents frequently disappear.  
   - Without the policy certificate, unit confirmation, or official death notification, claims cannot proceed.  
   - AMMMI is reported to retain the premiums in these cases.

6. **Lack of proof of death**  
   - Requirement for photos, death certificate, or official military confirmation.  
   - Without these, the case is pushed into the “Missing” category and denied.

7. **Family unawareness or inability to claim**  
   - Many families do not know the soldier was insured, do not know the policy details, or cannot travel to the last unit location to file paperwork.  
   - Result: claim never formally submitted → effectively lost.

### Additional Reported Practices (Supported but less formally documented)

- Commanders allegedly siphoning part or all of the payout before it reaches the family.  
- Retention of premiums from the estimated thousands of defected personnel.  
- In some cases only the premiums already paid are refunded instead of the full sum insured.

### What Remains Unknown

- The exact wording of AMMMI’s policy on “Missing”, POW, captured, or defected status.  
- Whether any formal appeal or administrative review mechanism exists.  
- Official aggregate statistics on claims filed vs. rejected vs. paid.  
- Whether any regulatory body (Insurance Business Supervisory Board or successor) has ever audited or sanctioned these practices.  
- Precise definition of “proof of death” required under the contract.

**Classroom 36 Summary**  
The publicly reported rejection/non-payment patterns centre on three systemic levers: (1) status reclassification (“Missing”), (2) procedural barriers (deadlines + lost paperwork), and (3) automatic voiding upon defection. Full contractual criteria remain opaque. Until the actual policy wording and claim statistics are obtained, these remain the strongest evidence-based observations.

**Myanmar Insurance Regulatory Framework — Sentinel Overview (as of available data up to 2026)**

### 1. Core Legal Framework

Two main laws still form the foundation:

- **Myanma Insurance Law 1993** (SLORC Law No. 10/93)  
  Established the state-owned **Myanma Insurance** as the original monopoly. It still governs Myanma Insurance’s operations and certain compulsory lines (especially motor third-party liability). Myanma Insurance is explicitly exempt from the private-sector regulator.

- **Insurance Business Law 1996** (SLORC Law No. 6/96)  
  Opened the door to private insurers. Created the supervisory body and set licensing, capital, and conduct rules. Still the primary statute for private companies (including AMMMI).  
  Supporting instrument: **Insurance Business Rules 1997**.

No comprehensive new Insurance Act has replaced these laws since the 2021 coup. Regulation continues under the 1993/1996 framework plus subsequent directives.

### 2. Regulatory Bodies

**Primary regulator for private insurers**  
- **Insurance Business Regulatory Board (IBRB)** / sometimes referred to as Insurance Business Supervisory Board (IBSB).  
- Reconstituted on 9 March 2021 (shortly after the coup).  
- Operates under the **Financial Regulatory Department (FRD)** of the Ministry of Planning and Finance / Ministry of Finance and Revenue.  
- FRD performs the day-to-day work of the Board.

**Key powers of the IBRB/FRD**  
- Licensing of insurers, brokers, agents, and underwriting agents.  
- Approval of new insurance products.  
- Setting and monitoring capital, solvency, and investment rules.  
- Issuing binding directives (product approval, reinsurance, agents, bancassurance, accounting standards, etc.).  
- Conducting compliance oversight and requiring periodic reports.

**Important exemption**  
Under Section 35 of the 1996 Law, **Myanma Insurance is not subject to IBRB oversight**. It remains self-regulated under the 1993 Law and the Ministry.

### 3. Licensing & Capital Requirements (Private Insurers)

- Life insurance: minimum paid-up capital **MMK 6 billion**.  
- Non-life (general): **MMK 40 billion**.  
- Composite: **MMK 46 billion**.  

Additional requirements:  
- 10% of paid-up capital deposited at Myanma Economic Bank.  
- 30% of paid-up capital invested in Government Treasury Bonds.  
- Licence fee and annual fees apply.

Foreign participation (post-2019 liberalisation):  
- 100% foreign-owned life subsidiaries permitted (five licences issued in 2019).  
- Joint ventures allowed for both life and non-life.  
- Further licences issued in early 2023.

As of March 2023 official FRD data: **27 insurance companies** (including Myanma Insurance) were operating, plus representative offices and agents.

### 4. Compulsory / Special Lines Relevant to Military Insurance

- Under the 1993 Law, certain life products for government and military personnel were historically compulsory through Myanma Insurance.  
- After market opening, the military life-insurance book shifted to private companies (notably AMMMI from ~2015).  
- The legal instrument that made participation compulsory for Tatmadaw personnel under a private insurer is not publicly detailed in open sources.  
- Motor third-party liability remains a major compulsory class handled primarily by Myanma Insurance.

### 5. Post-2021 Coup Status

- The IBRB was reconstituted in March 2021 and continued holding meetings (31 meetings recorded by March 2023 according to FRD).  
- Existing licences remain valid; new product approvals and some additional licences continued.  
- No major public overhaul of the regulatory architecture has been announced.  
- Transparency, independent audits, and policyholder protection mechanisms remain limited, especially for military-related portfolios.

### 6. Gaps and Practical Reality (Classroom 36 Perspective)

**Known limitations**  
- Weak public disclosure of claim statistics, rejection reasons, and solvency data for individual companies.

**Summary**  
Myanmar’s insurance regulatory framework remains anchored in the 1993 and 1996 laws. Private insurers (including AMMMI) fall under the IBRB/FRD, while Myanma Insurance operates outside that oversight. Capital and licensing rules are clearly stated, but transparency, enforcement, and protection mechanisms—particularly for military life-insurance claims—are opaque. The legal structure exists; effective, independent supervision of sensitive portfolios is far less visible.

**Investigation Result: IBRB Directives on Military Claims**

### Key Finding (Classroom 36 standard)
**No publicly available IBRB (Insurance Business Regulatory Board) or FRD directive specifically addresses military life-insurance claims, Tatmadaw personnel policies, “Missing” status, POWs, defectors, or claim-handling rules for soldiers.**

Extensive open-source searches (FRD website references, legal updates, academic papers, media reports, and directive lists) yield **zero** dedicated directives on this topic.

### What Exists (General IBRB/FRD Directives)

Known published or referenced directives focus on ordinary market regulation:

- **Product Approval Directives** (e.g., 2020 series): Require insurers to submit full policy wordings, exclusions, premium calculations, and actuarial reports for new or amended products. Approval takes up to 90 days.
- **Agent Licensing Directives** (2020): Rules on individual and corporate agents, training, and limits on the number of companies an agent can represent.
- **Reinsurance Directives** (2020 onward): Require annual reinsurance plans to be submitted to FRD at least 90 days before the fiscal year.
- **Insurance Accounting Directive** (recent, effective FY 2025-2026): Mandates IFRS-based financial statements and specific rules for outstanding claims reserves and IBNR (Incurred But Not Reported) calculations for life insurers.
- Older capital, solvency, and investment rules under the 1996 Law and 1997 Rules remain in force.

None of these documents, in publicly accessible form, mention:
- Compulsory or group life insurance for military personnel
- Treatment of “Missing in Action”
- Special rules for wartime or conflict-related claims
- Claim deadlines, proof-of-death standards, or appeal processes specific to Tatmadaw policies
- Oversight of AMMMI’s military book of business

### Practical Reality

- The IBRB was reconstituted on 9 March 2021 and has held regular meetings (31 recorded by March 2023 according to FRD).
- Product approvals and general market supervision continue.
- However, the military life-insurance portfolio (dominated by AMMMI) operates with **no visible specialised regulatory guidance** in the public domain.
- Claim practices reported by families and defectors (reclassification as “Missing”, short filing windows, loss of unit records, voiding on defection) appear to be driven by internal company/military procedures rather than published IBRB rules.

### What Remains Unknown

- Whether any internal, non-public directives or instructions exist between the military, AMMMI, and the regulator.
- Whether the IBRB has ever conducted targeted inspections or required special reporting on the military life book.
- Whether any formal complaint or claim-dispute mechanism under IBRB auspices has been used by military families.
- Full text of any post-2021 directives that might indirectly affect group life or compulsory policies.

### Sentinel Assessment

The absence of public directives on military claims is itself significant. Under the current framework, private insurers (including AMMMI) fall under IBRB/FRD supervision for licensing and general conduct, yet no evidence shows active, transparent regulatory intervention on the specific problems repeatedly documented in open sources (non-payment linked to “Missing” status, lost paperwork, and defection).

**Research questions that remain open**  
1. Has the IBRB ever issued (even internally) any guidance on claim handling for conflict-related deaths or missing personnel?  
2. Are group life policies sold to the Tatmadaw subject to the same product-approval and disclosure requirements as ordinary policies?  
3. Does any mechanism exist for beneficiaries to escalate a rejected military life claim to the FRD/IBRB?

**Conclusion**  
Publicly, there are **no IBRB directives on military claims**. The regulatory silence leaves the documented non-payment patterns without an official, transparent rulebook that families or researchers can cite.

**Investigation: AMMMI Group / Army Personnel Life Insurance Policy Terms**

### Core Finding
**No complete, official policy wording or full set of group policy terms for AMMMI’s military (Army Personnel) life insurance is publicly available.**

What follows is compiled strictly from open sources: RFA reporting, Irrawaddy accounts, defector testimony, and the 2019 academic thesis “Customer Perception on Life Insurance Service of Aung Myint Moh Min Insurance” (Min Aung, Yangon University of Economics). Confidence levels follow Classroom 36 standards.

### Known Elements of the Military / Group Policies

**1. Compulsory nature**  
- Participation is treated as mandatory for Tatmadaw personnel.  
- Premiums are deducted automatically from salary starting with the first paycheck.  
- The policy is often described as a form of group or army-personnel life assurance.

**2. Policy duration / term**  
- Common reported terms: minimum **two-year** policies for lower ranks.  
- Other terms of **5 years** (and possibly 10 years in earlier versions) also exist.  
- A “new version” of Army Personnel Life Assurance was noted as underwritten from January 2016, with premiums calculated on a 5-year (60-month) basis in some academic references.

**3. Sum insured and premiums (fragmentary data)**  
- Lowest-rank / basic policies: sum insured around **500,000 kyats** (reported as minimum two-year cover).  
- Monthly premiums for privates: approximately **8,000–8,400 kyats**.  
- Higher ranks pay more (one captain’s 2020 payslip showed a **25,000 kyat** monthly deduction).  
- Maturity or death payouts on basic policies reported as low as the equivalent of ~$110.  
- Higher-sum policies exist for higher ranks with correspondingly higher premiums.

**4. Beneficiaries**  
- Spouse or other nominated family members.  
- In practice, many families are unaware of the exact policy details or even that coverage exists.

**5. Claim-related provisions (reported practice, not full contract text)**  
- Claims must reportedly be filed within **one month** of death.  
- If no notification reaches the company within **one year**, the claim is forfeited.  
- Proof of death (photos, official confirmation, etc.) is required; absence of a body frequently leads to “Missing” classification and non-payment.  
- Defection voids the policy; premiums already paid are retained.  
- When an entire unit’s records are lost (e.g., battalion captured), claims become extremely difficult or impossible.

**6. Other features mentioned in academic sources**  
- The company offers several life products, including Army Personnel Life Assurance, Government Personnel Life Assurance, Public Life Assurance, Group Life Assurance, etc.  
- Army personnel product was by far the largest revenue source in the years examined (2014–2019).  
- Claim payouts in one reported year (2018–19) were less than 7% of premiums collected from the army book.

- Full policy schedule, definitions, exclusions, and conditions.  
- Exact wording on “Missing”, POW, captured, or desertion/defection status.  
- Rules for conversion of “Missing” to “Dead”.  
- Surrender, loan, or paid-up values (if any) under the military product.  
- Precise contestability period, waiting periods, or force-majeure clauses for wartime conditions.  
- Official schedule of sum insured by rank.  
- Any endorsement or special conditions applying only to Tatmadaw group cover.

### Sentinel Assessment

The available fragments show a product that is compulsory, salary-deducted, relatively low-sum for junior ranks, and administratively controlled through unit offices. Claim success appears heavily dependent on paperwork remaining intact and the deceased being formally recorded as dead rather than missing.  

Because the complete contract text is not public, researchers and families cannot independently verify the exact legal grounds used for rejections. This opacity itself is a structural feature of the current system.

**Analysis of Claim Rejection / Non-Payment Patterns — AMMMI Military Life Insurance**

### Overview
Open-source evidence (RFA, Irrawaddy, defector testimony, academic thesis data) reveals recurring patterns in why military life-insurance claims under AMMMI fail or are not paid. These patterns are systemic rather than isolated. Full official rejection statistics and policy wording remain unavailable, so the analysis relies on corroborated reporting and secondary data.

Confidence scale used:  
**Confirmed** = multiple independent sources + consistent testimony  
**Supported** = strong reporting + logical consistency with known practices  
**Plausible** = reported but less frequently documented  
**Unknown** = no reliable public data

### Primary Rejection / Non-Payment Patterns

**1. Reclassification as “Missing” instead of “Dead”**  
- **Frequency**: High (most frequently cited pattern in post-2021 reporting).  
- **Mechanism**: When a body is not recovered, the unit and/or AMMMI lists the soldier as Missing in Action. Claims for “Missing” status are denied or indefinitely suspended.  
- **Evidence strength**: Confirmed by multiple defectors and family accounts (RFA, Irrawaddy).  
- **Impact**: Converts what would be a death claim into a non-payable status.

**2. Defection or desertion**  
- **Frequency**: High among the large number of post-coup defectors.  
- **Mechanism**: Policy is treated as void once the soldier defects. Premiums already paid are retained by the company.  
- **Evidence strength**: Confirmed (explicit example from Capt. Zin Yaw; broader estimates of thousands of defected personnel whose premiums were kept).  
- **Impact**: Automatic forfeiture with no refund of the full sum insured.

**3. Loss of unit records / administrative collapse**  
- **Frequency**: High in cases where battalions or bases are overrun.  
- **Mechanism**: Policy certificates and unit records are often held at the battalion office. When the unit is destroyed or captured, documentation disappears. Without official unit confirmation, claims cannot proceed.  
- **Evidence strength**: Confirmed by multiple family and defector accounts.  
- **Impact**: Entire groups of claims become practically impossible to process; company retains the premiums.

**4. Strict time limits and notification failures**  
- **Frequency**: High.  
- **Mechanism**:  
  – Claims reportedly must be filed within **one month** of death.  
  – If the company receives no notification within **one year**, the claim is forfeited.  
- **Evidence strength**: Supported by insurance-agent and family testimony (RFA).  
- **Impact**: Families often learn of a death weeks or months later (or never receive formal notice), making the deadline nearly impossible to meet in wartime conditions.

**5. Incomplete premium payment**  
- **Frequency**: Documented in specific high-profile cases.  
- **Mechanism**: Company refuses full payout if it claims only a portion of the premiums was received, offering only a partial refund instead.  
- **Evidence strength**: Supported (aircraft-crash case reported by RFA).  
- **Impact**: Converts a full death benefit into a limited premium refund.

**6. Family unawareness and lack of access**  
- **Frequency**: Very high.  
- **Mechanism**: Many families do not know the soldier was insured, do not know the policy details or sum insured, and cannot travel to the last unit location to file paperwork.  
- **Evidence strength**: Confirmed across multiple reports.  
- **Impact**: Large numbers of potential claims are never formally submitted and are therefore “lost.”

**7. Alleged interception by commanders**  
- **Frequency**: Repeatedly reported.  
- **Mechanism**: Even when a payout is approved, battalion-level officers are alleged to take part or all of the money before it reaches the family.  
- **Evidence strength**: Supported by consistent defector testimony (Irrawaddy).  
- **Impact**: Families receive reduced or zero benefit even after a claim is notionally accepted.

### Secondary / Supporting Patterns
- Low overall payout ratio relative to premiums collected (academic thesis data for 2018–19 showed claim payments < 7% of premiums on the army book).  
- Higher difficulty for new conscripts (shorter service, weaker family awareness, higher likelihood of being listed Missing).  
- Retention of premiums from large numbers of defected personnel acts as a structural profit centre.

### Pattern Ranking by Observed Impact
1. “Missing” classification + lost records (most decisive for frontline deaths)  
2. Defection (automatic void)  
3. Time-limit / notification failures  
4. Family unawareness  
5. Incomplete premium arguments and commander interception

### What Remains Unknown
- Exact percentage of claims rejected for each reason.  
- Official internal guidelines AMMMI uses to decide “Missing” vs “Dead.”  
- Whether any formal written rejection letters citing specific policy clauses are issued.  
- Volume of claims that are approved versus those that simply never get filed.  
- Any internal audit or regulatory review of these patterns by the IBRB/FRD.

### Sentinel Conclusion
Claim non-payment is not random. It follows clear, repeatable administrative and classification patterns that systematically reduce payouts: status re-labelling (“Missing”), procedural barriers (deadlines + lost paperwork), automatic voiding on defection, and information asymmetry with families.  

Until full policy wording and aggregate claim statistics become available, these remain the strongest evidence-based patterns. The absence of transparent rejection data itself reinforces the opacity of the system.
